Bhakti Movements of India

Devotion sung in the language of the people, not the Sanskrit of the priests. It began with the Tamil Azhwars and Nayanmars, rose in Kannada and Marathi, and by the 15th and 16th centuries filled the north and east, from Kabir's Varanasi to Chaitanya's Bengal and Sankardev's Assam. Here are twenty-five of its voices, numbered in order of time.

Bhakti tells her own story

A verse in the Bhagavata Mahatmya of the Padma Purana gives Bhakti herself a voice, and she describes the very journey on this map:

उत्पन्ना द्राविडे साहं वृद्धिं कर्णाटके गता ।
क्वचित्क्वचिन्महाराष्ट्रे गुर्जरे जीर्णतां गता ॥
"I was born in the Dravida land and grew up in Karnataka. Here and there in Maharashtra I lived on, and in Gujarat I grew old." The verses that follow have her made young again in Vrindavan.

The Bhagavata itself (11.5.39) says that in the Kali age devotees of Narayana will appear "here and there, but in great numbers in the Dravida lands, where the Tamraparni flows". Historians tell the same story in their own terms: a devotional movement that began in the Tamil country between the 6th and 9th centuries and spread north over the next eight hundred years.

In order of time. The number on each card matches its marker on the map and its bar on the timeline.

c. 580–661 CE
1

Appar அப்பர்

Tamil · Shaiva · one of the four great Nayanmars

Born a Shaiva, he became a Jain monk, then returned to Shiva and spent his life walking from temple to temple, singing. His hymns fill three books of the Tevaram. With the Azhwars and the other Nayanmars he set the pattern the whole movement would follow: songs in the spoken language, sung at named shrines, open to all. The full Tamil story is on the Azhwars & Nayanmars page.

Andal ஆண்டாள் ♀

Tamil · Vaishnava · the only woman among the Azhwars

The foster daughter of the Azhwar Periyalvar, she would marry no one but Vishnu himself. Her Tiruppavai, thirty verses of a girl waking her friends for a dawn vow, is sung in homes and temples across South India every Margazhi. Her bridal longing for God is the first of many women's voices on this map. See the Azhwars & Nayanmars page.

3

Basavanna ಬಸವಣ್ಣ

Kannada · Shaiva · the Lingayat reformer

Treasurer to the Kalachuri king Bijjala at Kalyana, he turned his house into a meeting place for poets of every caste, the Anubhava Mantapa or hall of experience. He gave every devotee a personal linga to wear and taught that honest work (kayaka) is worship. His short free-verse vachanas, signed Kudalasangamadeva, mock the rich who build temples and call the devotee's own body the true shrine. An inter-caste marriage among his followers set off a violent backlash, and the community scattered.

Allama Prabhu ಅಲ್ಲಮಪ್ರಭು

Kannada · Shaiva · the mystic of the circle

The most inward of the vachana poets. Where Basavanna argues with society, Allama speaks in riddles about the emptiness at the heart of experience. Tradition makes him the presiding voice of the Anubhava Mantapa, testing newcomers, Akka Mahadevi among them. His signature name is Guheshvara, "lord of caves".

Akka Mahadevi ಅಕ್ಕಮಹಾದೇವಿ ♀

Kannada · Shaiva · "elder sister"

She took Shiva as her only husband, left a king's palace, and wandered with nothing but her long hair for clothing. At Kalyana she won the respect of Allama and Basavanna and the title Akka, elder sister. She ended her life in the forests of Kadali near Srisailam, whose Shiva she called Chennamallikarjuna, "Mallikarjuna the beautiful". About 350 vachanas carry her signature.

Jayadeva जयदेव

Sanskrit · Krishna · the poet of the Gita Govinda

The one Sanskrit poet on this map, included because his Gita Govinda, twelve chapters of songs on the love of Radha and Krishna, gave the eastern movements their language of longing. It is sung daily in the Jagannath temple at Puri and lies behind Odissi dance. He names his birthplace Kindubilva. Odisha identifies it with Kenduli Sasan near Puri; Bengal and Mithila claim villages of their own. Hymns attributed to him sit in the Guru Granth Sahib.

Namdev नामदेव

Marathi and Hindi · the Varkari who went north

A tailor's son from Narsi who sang to Vithoba at Pandharpur, then carried his songs to the north. He is the first bridge on this map: his Hindi hymns are in the Guru Granth Sahib, and Sikh tradition places his death at Ghuman in Punjab, though Maharashtra holds he rests at Pandharpur's temple step. Scholars dispute his dates; some place him a century later.

Jnaneshwar ज्ञानेश्वर

Marathi · the founder-saint of the Varkaris

The son of an excommunicated Brahmin, he wrote the Dnyaneshwari in 1290, a Marathi commentary on the Bhagavad Gita that brought its philosophy out of Sanskrit. He took living samadhi at Alandi at about twenty-one. Every year, lakhs of Varkari pilgrims walk from Alandi to Pandharpur carrying his sandals in a palanquin.

Lal Ded लल्लेश्वरी ♀

Kashmiri · Shaiva · the wandering yogini

A Kashmiri Pandit woman who left an unhappy marriage to wander as a yogini. Her four-line vakhs are rooted in Kashmir Shaivism and are still quoted in everyday Kashmiri speech. Hindus call her Lalleshwari, Muslims Lalla Arifa, and the Sufi Nund Rishi counted her his inspiration.

Ramananda रामानन्द

Hindi · Rama · the teacher of Varanasi

The hinge of the whole story in northern tradition. His lineage traces its teaching back to the south, and in Varanasi he took disciples of every caste and both sexes. Tradition names Kabir, Ravidas, Pipa, Sena and Dhanna among them, though historians doubt some of these links. His Ramanandi order is today one of India's largest. One hymn attributed to him is in the Guru Granth Sahib.

11

Kabir कबीर

Hindi · the weaver of Varanasi · nirguna

Raised in a family of Muslim weavers, he called God Ram but meant no idol and no temple, and he mocked pandit and mullah alike. His two-line dohas are among the most quoted verses in Hindi. Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs all claim him: his hymns are in the Guru Granth Sahib, and the Kabir Panth keeps his Bijak. He chose to die at Maghar, a town said to bring a bad rebirth, to prove that place did not matter.

Annamacharya అన్నమాచార్య

Telugu · Venkateswara · the first great composer

The singer of Lord Venkateswara. By tradition he composed 32,000 Telugu sankirtanas; thousands survive, engraved on copper plates kept at Tirumala. His songs are the oldest large body of compositions in what became Carnatic music.

Narsinh Mehta નરસિંહ મહેતા

Gujarati · Krishna · the first poet of Gujarat

Gujarati literature's Adi Kavi, a poor Nagar Brahmin of Junagadh whose caste fellows scorned his singing with people of every class. His Vaishnav Jan To, on the true devotee who "knows the pain of others", was Mahatma Gandhi's favourite bhajan and became a song of the freedom movement.

Srimanta Sankardev শংকৰদেৱ

Assamese · Krishna · saint, playwright and reformer

The maker of Assam's devotional culture. His Ekasarana Dharma, "shelter in the One", asked for devotion to Krishna alone, through his name, with no sacrifice and no caste bar. He wrote the first Borgeet songs on a pilgrimage to Badrinath, invented the Ankia Naat plays and the dance now called Sattriya, and left the village prayer halls (namghars) and monasteries (sattras) that still shape Assam.

Ravidas रविदास

Hindi · the cobbler-saint · nirguna

Born to a family of leather-workers, then treated as untouchable, he sang of a formless God before whom caste meant nothing. His Begampura hymn imagines a city "without sorrow", with no taxes and no second-class citizens. Forty of his hymns and a couplet are in the Guru Granth Sahib, and the Ravidassia community reveres him as its Guru. Some traditions name him Mirabai's teacher.

16

Guru Nanak ਗੁਰੂ ਨਾਨਕ

Punjabi · founder of Sikhism

The first Sikh Guru travelled for years across India and beyond, then settled at Kartarpur, where his followers farmed, prayed and ate together regardless of caste. Sikhs regard their faith as a revelation of its own, not a branch of Bhakti. Historians place him beside the Sant poets because they share one formless God, the power of the Name and a rejection of caste. In 1604 Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth, which holds the Gurus' hymns together with those of Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas and other bhagats from across India.

Surdas सूरदास

Braj Bhasha · the child Krishna

Remembered as a blind singer, he made the infant and boy Krishna of Braj live in Hindi: butter stolen, mothers scolding, gopis pining. The Sursagar carries his name, though many of its poems are later. Pushti Marg tradition makes him a disciple of Vallabhacharya and the foremost of eight poets of that sect; modern scholars doubt the link. Another tradition dates him 1483–1563.

Purandara Dasa ಪುರಂದರದಾಸ

Kannada · Haridasa · grandfather of Carnatic music

A rich merchant who gave everything away to sing as a wandering Haridasa, a "servant of Hari". A disciple of Vyasatirtha at Vijayanagara, he signed his songs Purandara Vittala and laid out the graded lessons every Carnatic student still begins with.

Chaitanya Mahaprabhu চৈতন্য মহাপ্রভু

Bengali · Krishna · the ecstatic of Nabadwip

A Sanskrit scholar who turned to Krishna and led crowds through the streets of Nabadwip chanting the divine names (sankirtana). He spent his last years at Puri, lost in the Gita Govinda and the Jagannath temple. His Gaudiya Vaishnavism, set down by his followers the Goswamis at Vrindavan, is the root of today's ISKCON.

Mirabai मीराबाई ♀

Rajasthani and Braj · Krishna as Giridhar Gopal

A Rathore princess of Merta who said she was already wedded to Krishna, the "mountain-lifter" Giridhar Gopal. Married into Mewar's royal house, she sang and danced with sadhus of every caste, and legend tells of the poison her in-laws sent her. She left for Vrindavan and ended her days at Dwarka. Few facts are certain, but her songs are sung everywhere in India.

Kanaka Dasa ಕನಕದಾಸ

Kannada · Haridasa · the shepherd-chieftain

A Kuruba (shepherd) chieftain of Bada who became, like Purandara, a disciple of Vyasatirtha. Refused entry to the Krishna temple at Udupi, he sang outside. Legend says the wall cracked and the image turned to face him; the window there is still called Kanakana Kindi. His Ramadhanya Charite stages a debate between ragi and rice that the poor man's grain wins.

Tulsidas तुलसीदास

Awadhi · Rama · the poet of the Ramcharitmanas

A Ramanandi monk who began the Ramcharitmanas at Ayodhya on Rama Navami in 1574 and finished it at Varanasi. Written in Awadhi rather than Sanskrit, it became the Ramayana of the Hindi heartland and the script of every village Ramlila. The Hanuman Chalisa is attributed to him. Popular tradition gives his birth as 1497; most scholars prefer about 1532. His telling is compared with Valmiki's and Kamban's on the Ramayana Journey page.

Marathi · the scholar-householder of Paithan

He carried the Varkari line between Jnaneshwar and Tukaram. Finding the Dnyaneshwari corrupted by copyists, he edited the text that is still read. He wrote a Marathi Bhagavata and lively bharuds, dramatic songs in the voices of ordinary people, to teach devotion and mock caste pride.

Poonthanam പൂന്താനം

Malayalam · Guruvayurappan

A Namboodiri of Keezhattoor whose infant son died on the day of his first-rice feast. In his grief he turned to Krishna at Guruvayur and wrote the Jnanappana, "the song of wisdom": 360 lines in a simple folk metre on how quickly life passes. Legend says Guruvayurappan preferred his plain Malayalam to the learned Sanskrit of his contemporary Melpathur.

Tukaram तुकाराम

Marathi · the crown of the Varkaris

A shopkeeper of Dehu ruined by famine, he gave himself to Vithoba and to song. Brahmins who objected made him sink his notebooks in the Indrayani river; after thirteen days, the story goes, they floated back. His thousands of abhangs, collected as the Tukaram Gatha, are the Varkari pilgrims' marching songs to this day. He disappeared in 1649 or 1650.

Two ways of loving God

Scholars of North Indian bhakti sort its poets into two streams. The line is not hard: Namdev and Tulsidas both sang of the Name, and Kabir called his formless God Ram.

CriterionSaguna — God with formNirguna — God beyond form
God isA person with a story: Rama, Krishna, Vithoba, ShivaThe One without qualities, found within; often still called Ram or Hari
WorshipImages, temples, pilgrimage, festivals, the deity's lilaThe Name, the true guru, inner listening; temples and rituals often mocked
FeelingLove as servant, friend, parent or belovedUnion and awakening; love as longing for the hidden Beloved
Voices hereSurdas, Mirabai, Tulsidas, Chaitanya, the Varkaris, the HaridasasKabir, Ravidas; Guru Nanak and the Sikh Gurus stand close

What they shared

The mother tongue

Tamil, Kannada, Marathi, Hindi, Bengali, Assamese: the songs left Sanskrit behind so that anyone could sing them.

Saints of every caste

A tailor (Namdev), a weaver (Kabir), a cobbler (Ravidas), a shepherd-chief (Kanaka), a shopkeeper (Tukaram), beside Brahmins and princes.

Women's voices

Andal, Akka Mahadevi, Lal Ded and Mirabai on this map, and many more, like the Varkari servant Janabai.

Singing together

Kirtan, bhajan, abhang, kirtana: devotion as group singing, often on the road, as in the Varkari walk to Pandharpur.

The Name

Repeating the divine name is enough, say saints as far apart as Chaitanya, Kabir, Sankardev and Tukaram.

Love over ritual

Most of them doubted sacrifice, caste purity and bookish learning, and some said so sharply.
