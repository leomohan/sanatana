The Trinity of Tiruvarur

Three composers born within thirteen years of each other in the same temple town. Their songs are still the core of every concert.

Tyagaraja

1767–1847 · the singer of Rama

Born Kakarla Tyagabrahmam into a Telugu family, he spent most of his life in Thiruvaiyaru on the Kaveri, singing to Rama. Tradition says he turned down a court appointment rather than sing for a king. His songs speak directly to God, often in the first person, and gave many ragas their lasting shape.

Muthuswami Dikshitar

1775–1835 · the scholar-poet

A veena player who studied for years in Varanasi, Dikshitar travelled from shrine to shrine and composed in Sanskrit for the deity of each. His kritis are slow and stately, dense with temple lore. He named ragas by the older scheme of Venkatamakhin. He even set Sanskrit words to Western tunes he heard from military bands.

Syama Sastri

1762–1827 · the poet of Kamakshi

The eldest of the three, born Venkata Subrahmanya into a family of priests who served the goddess Bangaru Kamakshi and had carried her image south from Kanchi. He settled with her in Thanjavur. He composed fewer songs than the other two, about three hundred, but his rhythmic craft is unmatched.

Language
Telugu, also Sanskrit and Tamil

Before the Trinity

Annamacharya

1408–1503 · Tirumala

The earliest major composer in the tradition, a singer of Lord Venkateswara at Tirumala. By tradition he composed 32,000 songs (sankirtanas) in Telugu. Thousands survive, engraved on copper plates kept at the temple.

TeluguVenkateswaraCopper plates

Purandara Dasa

1484–1564 · the grandfather of Carnatic music

A wealthy merchant who gave everything away to become a wandering Haridasa singer. He wrote in Kannada with the signature Purandara Vittala. He also laid out the graded exercises that every student still begins with, set in Mayamalavagowla (No. 15 below). For this he is called the Pitamaha, the grandfather.

KannadaHaridasaFirst lessons

Kshetrayya

c. 17th century · the padam

A master of the padam, a slow love song in Telugu addressed to Krishna as Muvva Gopala. Dancers still use his padams for expressive dance (abhinaya).

TeluguPadamDance

Arunachala Kavi

1711–1779 · the Rama Natakam

One of the Tamil Trinity, with Muthu Thandavar and Marimutthu Pillai, who composed in Tamil before the great three. His Rama Natakam tells the whole Ramayana in songs, drawing on Kamban. It is still sung in concerts. See the Ramayana journey.

After the Trinity

Swathi Thirunal

1813–1846 · the composer-king

Maharaja of Travancore, who in a short life composed in Sanskrit, Malayalam, Telugu, Kannada and Hindustani. His court drew musicians from across the south.

TravancoreMany languages

Gopalakrishna Bharati

1811–1896 · Nandanar Charitram

His Nandanar Charitram tells in Tamil songs the life of Nandanar, one of the sixty-three Nayanmars. Nandanar was born outcaste and longed to see the dancing Shiva of Chidambaram.

TamilNandanarMusical drama

Papanasam Sivan

1890–1973 · the Tamil Tyagaraja

He wrote hundreds of Tamil kritis that now stand beside the Telugu classics in every concert, and he also composed for early Tamil cinema. Admirers called him the Tamil Tyagaraja.

Tamil kritisFilm music

M. Balamuralikrishna

1930–2016 · composer in all 72

A child prodigy who had composed in all 72 melakartas by the age of fifteen. He went on to invent new ragas of only three or four notes, such as Lavangi and Mahati, and to bring the viola into Carnatic music.

Where 72 scales come from

A melakarta is a parent scale that uses all seven swaras once each, going up and coming down. There are only so many ways to choose them.

Sa and Pa are fixed

The tonic Sa and the fifth Pa never change. Every scale keeps both.

Ri and Ga: six pairs

Ri and Ga have three positions each but share pitches, so they can be paired six ways: R1 G1, R1 G2, R1 G3, R2 G2, R2 G3, R3 G3. These are the six rows of each half below.

Da and Ni: six pairs

Da and Ni pair up the same six ways. These are the six columns.

Ma: two choices

Shuddha Ma (M1) gives scales 1–36 and Prati Ma (M2) gives 37–72. So 6 × 6 × 2 = 72.

🔢 The number is in the name. Each name encodes its own number by the katapayadi system, which gives every consonant a digit. Take the first two syllables, read the digits, and reverse them. Ha-rikambhoji gives 8 and 2, so 28. Me-chakalyani gives 5 and 6, so 65. The scheme of 72 was worked out by Venkatamakhin in the 17th century. The names used today, with complete scales, were standardised after him and are associated with Govindacharya.

The 72 Melakartas

Rows are the twelve chakras and columns the Da–Ni pairs. Every scale in a row shares Ri, Ga and Ma; every scale in a column shares Da and Ni. ★ marks a few you will hear often. Hover over one to see why.
